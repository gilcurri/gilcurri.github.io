Barclays —
Savings Goals

What I did

Design sprints

Prototyping

User journey mapping

User testing

Wireframing



Team

In collaboration with Product managers and Design Principal at Barclays, and User researchers at EY Seren.


End User

Customers taking first steps toward saving regularly, and those with slightly more established savings habits.


Key insights

Barclays had identified a large portion of customers who were accruing money in their current account, without using any specific savings products.


This could make it difficult to keep savings and spending money separate, and mean missing out on the benefits that come with more tailored accounts, such as earning interest or getting cash back on purchases.

They wanted to help users think about their savings habits/targets more clearly, and enable them to plan for the future and make the most of other products that might benefit them.

They were also aware of competitor products and ‘challenger banks’ that helped users create savings targets and set up regular saving patterns and rules, and wanted to provide a similar service for their customers.

Defining the problem

These insights and aims led to the idea of a 'savings goals' product offering, which would enable users to set goals, track saving progress, and set up automatic rules to make day to day saving easier – all from within their banking app.



Developing potential solutions

We began by sketching out how the basic goal setting steps might work, in quick ideation sessions involving various team members.

These sessions soon raised questions around how the underlying product would work. After meetings with the Barclays research and product teams, and wider research of the consumer financial services market, we established that there were 3 main ways the goals product could work.



Partition an existing account

At the most basic, least intrusive level, users could save money within their existing current account, by marking certain amounts as being saved for a specific goal – no new accounts, direct debits, or standing orders required – by creating a 'partition'.

Create a pot

With slightly more upfront input required from the user, they could create a 'pot' within their existing current account – this could in actual fact create a new sort code and account number for this goal, in as streamlined a way possible. They could then move money into this pot, and it wouldn't be available for them to spend in their current account.

Open a new account

At the highest level, the user could opt to open a new account specifically tailored to the goal they were saving for – For example, high-interest, fixed term accounts for long-term goals, instant access ISAs for short term goals.

Immediately many complexities arose.

As users were known to be accruing money in their C/A, should we ask if some of that money was for the goal they were setting up?

As we were intending to track goal progress, would we need input from users each time money was added to their account?

For users that have other accounts with Barclays, would we need them to identify part or all of these balances as for particular goals? And how could we track saving progress across different accounts?

I started to explore how to visualise the concept of goal setting and tracking, and related features, by sketching out some early UI components. I also began to think about transitions between key screens, and how users would be able to track and interact with their progress via the app.

As we were focusing on prototyping using Barclays' existing UI kit, these scamps helped me think about how the kit might be developed to include new components, and where else they might be of value in Barclays product ecosystem.

It's clear from these early prototypes that communicating the difference between the various options was quite complex. Given the short timescales we were working with, we decided to test the function of 'partitions' separately. However, we'd received poor feedback from early test subjects, who said the 'partitioning' concept and language was difficult to understand. The concept of allocating money to a goal came up repeatedly and so was included in the next prototype.

We were keen to identify whether this could be a potential product option early on, wanting to assess how it sat within people's current mental models of saving and savings products, and also to identify pain points and complexities, particularly when dealing with multiple goals and accounts.

Barclays were also keen to progress the user profile to a customer with a current account and a savings account, as the goals project was merged with another workstream dealing with older users.

Results

The second prototype (example screens above) was tested in the EY Seren lab with a sample of some regular and some irregular savers, between the ages of 23 and 34.

Although allocation was intended to be the simplest route to saving for a goal, it involved many complexities:

No barrier between the allocated amount and the rest of the account balance meant it was too easy to eat into – users wanted some friction to stop spending their saved money.

Having to mark money as 'for' a certain goal each time it was deposited involved as much effort from the user as moving it to a separate 'pot' or account.

When spending from the account ate into the allocated amount, what happened when there was more than one? Prioritising solely by date felt restrictive, we needed to allow the user to prioritise their goals, adding more steps into the process.

Outcome

The images above show the wealth of insights gathered from the second round of testing, and the summary report from EY Seren confirmed many of our observations.

Rather than create new terminology and product functions that required change of users mental models, it would be better to focus on the process of opening and labeling accounts.

We suggested a better option would be to streamline the account opening process, and do as much of it behind the scenes as possible – in a similar way to how goals within Monzo's platform work.

Another key takeaway was that when it comes to saving, users do like to have some friction between their saved and spending money – even when they initially state otherwise.

Although the prototype tested didn't make any further progress into production, there were many useful outcomes from the project, and it provided a valuable base for further product development.
